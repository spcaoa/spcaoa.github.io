The gap, and two ways to close it

The shortfall and the plan

We spend more than we collect. This page shows how much, what is being done about costs, and the three options the Managing Committee is putting to owners.

How big is the gap?

At this year's rate of spending, the association is about ₹84 L a year short. Costs are being cut — about ₹20 L a year, most of it already done by reducing the number of security guards — which brings it to ₹64 L. That has to come from maintenance.

Each option, in plain words

Carry on as we are. The reserves keep paying the difference — about ₹64 L a year after the savings. The interest they earn is already spent; this eats the principal. At that rate, the money set aside for lifts, painting and waterproofing is gone in about fifteen years, and the estate has nothing when those bills come.

The Finance Sub-Committee's 10.1%. The Finance Sub-Committee, the resident owners who reviewed last year's accounts, proposed 10.1%. It does not work, for two separate reasons, and it is worth being precise about both. First, their 10.1% was on the bill including GST. For the 148 flats that cross ₹7,500, that GST is inside the new amount, so the association's own share goes down: an A-type flat would pay ₹8,183, of which ₹1,248 is GST, leaving ₹6,934 for the association against ₹7,432 today. Across the estate the association ends up about ₹2 L a year better off — not ₹23 L. (If GST applied only above ₹7,500, it would be about ₹22 L.) Second, their gap of ₹23 L was small because it left out one-off repairs, counted ₹18 L of savings before any had been made, and put tax at a third of what is actually paid. Even 10.1% on the base charge would raise ₹23 L of the ₹64 L needed.

Option A: a 23% increase. Raises ₹52 L. The monthly account is still about ₹12 L a year short on paper; if one-off repairs ease next year, that closes. Nothing is set aside for the future. The A- and B-type flats cross ₹7,500 and pay GST; the C, D and D1 flats do not.

Option B: 23%, plus a one-time ₹1 lakh from each flat into the reserves. The ₹1 lakh is not spent. It goes into fixed deposits, and the interest — about ₹12 L a year after tax — closes the gap. The ₹2.9 Cr also puts back what was taken out of the reserves over the last two years. Collected over about six months from November. Whether GST applies to it is being checked.

Option C: a 39% increase, nothing one-time. Raises ₹86 L. This is the only way to both balance the account and rebuild the reserves out of maintenance alone, and it is close to what the July meeting was asked for and turned down. The cost is the GST line: at 39% the C and D1 flats cross ₹7,500 too, so 286 of the 294 flats pay 18% GST — for a C-type that is about ₹1,515 a month going to the government on top of the increase. Option B reaches the same place for 23% and a one-time payment.

Either way, this financial year still needs about ₹75 L from the reserves, because a new rate would only apply from October. The increase fixes next year, not this one.

Why 23%?

Two reasons. It is the most that can be asked before the C-type flats cross ₹7,500 a month, which is where 18% GST starts. The A- and B-type flats cross that line at any increase at all — that is the tax law's threshold, not the association's choice — but going past 23% would put three-quarters of the estate into GST for no gain to the association, because the GST goes to the government. And 23% is close enough that the ₹1 lakh option, or a good year on repairs, balances it.

Why not 15% plus ₹1 lakh? Because of tax. At 15% the account is ₹30 L a year short, and the ₹1 lakh from each flat earns only ₹12 L a year after the association's 39% tax on interest. The other ₹18 L would have to be taken out of the reserves every year. For 15% to work without touching the reserves, the one-time amount would need to be about ₹2.4 lakh per flat.

What it means for your flat

Once a flat's monthly charge crosses ₹7,500, 18% GST applies. The tax department says it applies to the whole amount; a High Court has said only to the part above ₹7,500. The difference is about ₹1,300 a month for the larger flats, and the association is taking written advice on which applies.

Why not just cut costs?

Both are happening. The facility and security contracts have never been put out to tender; they are being tendered now, and ₹15 L a year has already been saved on guards. But the two together are ₹1.8 Cr a year, and residents rated housekeeping and gardening the best things about living here. Cutting those to avoid an increase would trade a visible service for an invisible saving.
